lapstack
Render a second stacked image from the focus depth map: each pixel blends the frames nearest its depth. DFR will be shown side by side with LAP. Increases run time by ~25%.
Zerene's slabbing: the stack is cut into overlapping slabs, each fused on its own (LAP), and DFR blends the slab images instead of the frames — a pixel takes the slab(s) whose frames hold its depth. Fine detail and crossing structures come from LAP within a slab; the far-out-of-focus frames that build noise and halos over a whole stack never blend in. Frames in the overlaps are decoded once per slab.

Metadata
—
The EXIF is rebuilt with the camera, lens, exposure, date and orientation tags (MakerNote and thumbnail left out) and Software set to lapstack. PNG carries it as eXIf, iCCP and iTXt chunks, JPEG as APP1 / APP2 segments. Maps and animations get none.
Animations
—
Stereo and rocking
The depth map makes the stacked image a relief, and a view from the side is that relief sheared: each pixel slides sideways in proportion to its depth. The shift is how far the far end of the stack moves relative to the near end, as a percentage of the width (Zerene's maximum X shift; ±3 % suits most subjects, more gives a deeper but harder-to-fuse pair — for a scene d deep and w wide, a viewing angle a is a shift of tan(a) · d / w). The middle of the stack stays put. Where a near edge slides off the background, the background shows through. The pair is saved at the crop's size (each view the image's size), the rocking as a GIF at the animation size and speed, one sine cycle of the views per loop. If the depth looks inside out, the frames went back to front: untick the box.
The shear needs only the stacked image and its depth map, so it is instant, but a depth map is one surface: it cannot show what is behind a hair or a bristle. The refold does what Zerene does — it reads the frames again and fuses the stack once per view with every frame shifted by its index, so each view is a real LAP stack with no depth map involved. That costs one pass over the frames (as long as the run's fold) per batch of views: the stereo pair is one batch at full resolution, and a rocking sequence at the animation size fits in one batch on most GPUs. Its price is the method's halo: beside a near object, the far frames — which win there because the background is sharp in them — carry that object's defocused copy, displaced by the shift, so a soft ghost shows next to it, wider with a larger shift and a deeper scene. The file thumbnails always use the shear.
3D model
—
Helicon's 3D model: the depth map makes the stacked image a relief, and the model is that relief as a mesh — a grid of vertices over the image, each raised by the depth there, with the stacked image as its texture (the LAP or DFR image chosen under Stereo and rocking, whose near-end switch also says which way the relief stands). The relief is the depth of the stack as a percentage of the image width, the one thing the depth map cannot know: measure the subject, or set it by eye. Each vertex takes the mean depth of the pixels around it, so a finer mesh follows finer detail and a coarser one is smoother; a step in depth becomes a wall between the near surface and the far one, since a relief shows nothing behind an edge. The texture is 8-bit in the image format above (JPEG at its quality, or PNG), capped at the texture long edge because many viewers refuse textures over 8192 px; the model is cropped like the other files, x runs along the width (1 unit), y up, z towards the viewer. GLB opens in Windows 3D Viewer, macOS Quick Look, Blender and any web viewer; OBJ is what Helicon writes (the browser may ask to allow the three downloads); STL is for printing and has no texture. No content credentials are attached to 3D files.
Content credentials
A signed C2PA manifest is embedded in each saved file (contentcredentials.org). The certificate is self-signed and made in this browser: verifiers show the manifest, its assertions and the content hash as valid, and the signer as unknown. The key never leaves this browser.
